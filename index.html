Our mission

We build software for the people who keep cities moving — and we prove it works before they have to depend on it.

Who we build for

Riders, drivers and couriers, most of them working across several platforms at once on a mid-range phone and a prepaid data plan. That constraint shapes every decision: a 7 MB download, sixteen languages at full parity, and an app that keeps accepting work when the signal drops.

How we prove it

We built our own testing platform, because the bugs that matter in a multi-user product cannot be found by one person. It found six in our own finished app — the last of them on the run meant to sign the work off. We publish that, and the limits of it, rather than the version that flatters us.

What we stand behind

three commitments, each with a mechanism behind it.

A promise nobody can check is marketing. Each of these is held up by something in the code that fails loudly if it stops being true.

01

Free for workers, permanently

Not a trial and not a freemium tier. Buzz Buzz costs a driver nothing and always will.

The mechanism: the business model does not route through them. Revenue comes from enterprise supply, fleet APIs and workforce analytics — the shape that funded LinkedIn and Waze — so there is never a quarter in which charging drivers becomes the obvious fix.

02

Privacy the database enforces

One driver cannot read another’s data even if the app asks it to, and phone numbers are not exposed to other users at all.

The mechanism: 48 row-level-security policies across 17 tables, applied at the database rather than in the interface, plus a column-level grant that leaves the phone column genuinely unreadable. A migration assertion fails the deploy if that ever stops holding.

03

We publish the misses

Every number we show says what it measured and, just as plainly, what it did not.

The mechanism: it is built into the tooling. A fresh Populace adapter reports 2/13 coverage and refuses to run rather than passing while testing nothing, and any single failure is a finding — there is no tolerance band a real bug can hide beneath. The report above says correctness run, not a load test for the same reason.

A gig driver's day is measured in kilometres, fuel and hours away from home. Buzz Buzz answers the question that actually matters at the end of it: was today worth it?

01

Earnings that follow the road, not the clock

Money accrues from distance genuinely travelled. Sitting in traffic doesn't quietly inflate your total, because a stationary phone earns nothing.

GPS is noisy at rest — a phone on a dashboard drifts tens of metres while parked. Movement under 15 metres is discarded as drift, and any jump implying more than 200 km/h is rejected as a bad fix. What remains is distance you actually covered. Your rate per kilometre is yours to set, and totals reset at local midnight server-side, so yesterday's numbers never linger on someone else's screen.

02

A map with people on it

See the drivers you know moving in real time, and search any city, neighbourhood or shop on earth.

Friends' positions refresh every ten seconds — a driver who joins mid-shift appears without anyone restarting the app. Search runs two queries at once, one biased to where you are and one worldwide, then ranks settlements above side-streets, so "Jakarta" returns the city and "7-Eleven" returns the branch down the road. Sharing your position is off until you turn it on, and switching it off deletes your location rather than merely hiding it.

03

Fluent wherever the work is

Sixteen languages and twenty-three currencies, chosen for you from where you actually are.

English, Filipino, Spanish, Hindi, Bengali, Indonesian, Malay, Thai, Vietnamese, Portuguese, French, German, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Arabic — with the entire interface mirrored right-to-left for Arabic, not merely translated. The app reverse-geocodes your first GPS fix to a country and picks your currency from a map of forty-nine of them. Language is yours to choose rather than guessed at, and the currency it picked is yours to override.

04

Messaging that behaves like messaging

Direct and group conversations with delivery and read receipts, presence, and last-seen.

Messages appear the instant you send them rather than waiting on the next poll. A single tick becomes a double tick when the recipient's device fetches it, then turns blue when they open the conversation. Photos attach from the camera or the gallery, and conversations open at the newest message.

05

It keeps working underground

Basement car parks, lifts, tunnels, dead cells. Nothing a driver writes is lost to a missing signal.

A post or a message sent with no connection is held on the device, survives the app being closed, and is sent the moment there is signal again — marked "waiting to send" in the meantime rather than quietly disappearing. Photos travel as links to object storage instead of inside the message, so a feed of twenty pictures costs a fraction of what it used to on a metered plan, and the full image is fetched only when someone taps it.

06

The people working your streets

A community of drivers sharing what's actually happening out there — flooding, surges, closures, queues.

Posts with photos, likes, comments and friend requests. Groups carry real membership counts rather than invented ones. Weekly distance and earnings challenges run against a leaderboard of drivers you know, resetting on a genuine schedule instead of a decorative one.

07

Privacy that is structural, not promised

Location is collected only while tracking is on. Never in the background. Never with the app closed.

Every table enforces access per-account at the database itself, so one driver cannot read another's data even if the app asked it to. Phone numbers are never exposed to other users. Deleting your account removes your profile, settings, posts, messages and location history — through the same path we test automatically before every release.

First run against a real, finished app · 9 August 2026

We pointed Populace at Buzz Buzz — our own Android product, already signed, already through a full manual test of every screen. Six simulated drivers across Manila and Mumbai found five bugs in three and a half minutes. One of them meant nobody could create a working account. A sixth was caught later, on the run whose only job was to confirm there was nothing left.

What it found, and why nobody had seen it

01

Signing up created no profile — every new user was broken

A privacy fix that morning had restricted writes on the profiles table to a named list of columns, so phone numbers could never be read by another user. The signup code used an upsert. In Postgres, INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE needs SELECT on every column it touches — and one of those columns was, deliberately, unreadable. The write was refused. Silently.

Why one person never sees it: your own account already exists. The bug only fires on a fresh signup. Populace creates six brand-new accounts every run and hit it within fifteen seconds.

02

Likes bounced back, roughly one tap in four

The likes table has an insert policy and a delete policy and — by design — no UPDATE policy. The app upserted here too, so the second like on a post became ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE and was refused.

Why one person never sees it: with one account and one thumb you rarely double-like anything. Six people liking each other's posts against a 2.5-second feed poll do it constantly.

03

Editing a profile failed the same way

Same upsert, same unreadable column, same silent refusal.

04

Two faults in Populace's own reference adapter

One passed an argument under the wrong name. The other ignored the error returned by a write — so a failure that happened during signup was reported against a later call, turning one cause into three confusing symptoms.

We are telling you this on purpose. An unchecked error is the exact fault this product exists to catch, and it was in our code. Making that one line throw changed the report from “post failed 14 times” to “profile row not created: permission denied”.

You cannot upsert a column you cannot select. Three of the five were that one mistake wearing different clothes — invisible in code review, invisible in a single-user walkthrough, and obvious within seconds to six users signing up at once.

Six users for three minutes is a correctness run, not a load test, and the project was empty at the time. Populace has been pointed at one real backend so far and that backend was ours. The next one should be yours.

01

One adapter is the entire integration

Thirteen small methods, two of them required. Each answers a single question: how does this happen in my app?

The engine knows how to be a person — where they go, how chatty they are, when they take a break. It knows nothing about your product, and that separation is enforced rather than aspirational. Implement createUser and deleteUser and you have a working run. A read-only service can implement three methods and still get something useful. Anything you leave out is reported as untested — never quietly counted as passing.

02

Through the front door, always

Every action goes through the same API your customers hit, as a genuinely separate authenticated identity.

No admin keys. No service-role credentials. No direct database writes. A simulation that bypasses your permission rules proves nothing about whether those rules work — and permission bugs are precisely what a multi-user simulation is best at finding, because they only appear when one identity reaches for another's data. Account deletion runs through your own delete path, so the route almost nobody tests gets exercised on every run.

03

People, not a load generator

Each simulated person carries their own habits, and everyone acts at once.

Postiness, chattiness, break frequency and travel speed vary between individuals, because identical agents find identical bugs. They move along real looping routes through five cities and talk in language that suits each one. Crucially they act concurrently — sequential agents never surface a race condition, which is half the reason to run a simulation at all.

04

Evidence, not vibes

Every run ends with a report you can hand to someone who wasn't watching.

Latency percentiles for every endpoint under concurrent load. Failures grouped by shape rather than exact text, so one bug is one line instead of fifty. An honest account of what was never exercised. Written as JSON for your pipeline and as a self-contained page for everyone else, with a non-zero exit code when problems are found. There is deliberately no tolerance threshold — a single failure among thousands is still a failure.

05

It refuses to touch production

Three independent refusals, any one of which stops a run.

The environment must declare itself non-production — opted into, never assumed. Your production hosts are matched against every string in the configuration however deeply nested, and no flag overrides that. An empty denylist warns loudly, because forgetting to fill it in is the likeliest version of this mistake. Simulated people appearing to real customers is deception, not testing.

06

It reports its own failures too

If the simulation breaks, the report says so instead of blaming your API.

Failures originating in your app and failures originating in Populace are tracked separately. When something breaks inside the tool itself, the report states plainly that the run did not test what it claims to have tested. A testing tool that can fail silently and still print a clean result is worse than no tool at all.
